Most decisions get judged by their expected outcome, but the *shape* of the payoff matters more than its average. Two bets can share the same expected value and be worlds apart: one costs you a little when it fails and pays a lot when it works; the other does the reverse. Asymmetry of risk is the habit of looking at the whole distribution — how much you can lose versus how much you can win, and with what probability — instead of collapsing it to a single number. It's the spine of Nassim Taleb's *Incerto*, where it goes by names like convexity, optionality, and antifragility.

The first rule that falls out of this is *avoid ruin*. A downside that ends the game has to be refused almost regardless of how unlikely it is, because you can't play tomorrow if you're wiped out today — losses aren't ergodic, so an average that includes a path to zero is a lie. Russian roulette has fine odds on any single pull and a catastrophic, unrecoverable tail; no expected-value calculation redeems it. Leverage, single points of failure, and unhedged catastrophic exposure all share this property: the rare outcome is the only one that ends up mattering.

The mirror image is where the real money is: *positive* asymmetry. Seek bets with a small, known, capped downside and a large or open-ended upside — what Taleb calls convexity, and the engine behind his barbell strategy (mostly safe, a sliver in convex long shots). Venture investing is this made into a business: you can only lose 1x your check but can make 100x, so you optimize for not missing the winners, and a handful of them pay for a sea of zeros.

Antifragility is the extreme case, and worth separating out. Taleb's triad: the *fragile* is harmed by volatility and shocks (a porcelain cup, an over-leveraged balance sheet, a just-in-time supply chain with no slack); the *robust* merely withstands them (a rock, the stoic); the *antifragile* actually *gains* from them. Muscles and bones rebuild stronger under load; the immune system learns from small insults (hormesis); evolution improves a population precisely through the stress that kills its weakest members. Mechanically it's the same convexity as before — because the payoff curve bends upward, more variance *raises* the expected result, so you want more disorder, not less.

The subtle part: antifragility at one level is usually paid for by fragility at the level below. Individual restaurants must fail for the restaurant *sector* to keep improving; organisms die so the species can adapt. Which means suppressing all volatility — bank bailouts, forest-fire suppression, helicopter parenting — doesn't remove risk, it stores it, trading frequent small shocks for rare catastrophic ones. Stability bought by banning volatility is often the most fragile state of all.

**Why it matters:** before sizing any bet, separate "what's the expected value?" from "what happens in the tails?" Refuse any downside you can't recover from, pay up for upside that's capped on the loss and open-ended on the gain — and prefer things that *feed* on volatility over things that merely survive it.
